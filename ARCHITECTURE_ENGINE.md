# ARCHITECTURE_ENGINE.md — The Executable Architecture Manual

**Authorship: Fable 5, legacy extraction, 2026-07-09. Content preserved verbatim per the Legacy Extraction Protocol.**

**What this is.** The machinery I run when I design software systems — written as procedures another AI can execute. It is strictly additive to the prior extractions and cites them instead of repeating them: **[M#]** points to [FABLE-MENTAL-MODELS.md](FABLE-MENTAL-MODELS.md); **FABLE §N** to [FABLE.md](FABLE.md); **DE §N** to [DECISION_ENGINE.md](DECISION_ENGINE.md); **PE §N** to [PLANNING_ENGINE.md](PLANNING_ENGINE.md); **RE §N** to [RESEARCH_ENGINE.md](RESEARCH_ENGINE.md). What this document adds is everything those left unsaid: the thinking that precedes the seams, the interface and data disciplines, the evolution machinery, failure-oriented design, and the new discipline of AI-native architecture.

**The one-sentence version.** Architecture is the publishing of constraints — a small set of decisions, made once and enforced structurally, inside which thousands of future decisions become easy, safe, and locally reversible.

# 1. Architecture Philosophy

**1.1 — What architecture is.** Three complementary definitions, each operational:

- **The irreversible subset.** Architecture is the set of decisions that will be expensive to reverse (FABLE §14) — which means the architect's first job is not designing things but *sorting* them: which decisions are one-way doors [M11] and must be made carefully, and which are furniture [M6] that implementation can decide freely. Most of what gets debated in "architecture reviews" is furniture; most of what actually constrains the next decade (data formats, identity, trust boundaries) slides through unexamined. The sorting *is* the skill.
- **The published physics.** Architecture is the set of rules the rest of the system is built *inside* — what components may know about each other, where state may live, which direction dependencies may point. Like physics, it works only if it is enforced by structure rather than vigilance [M12]: a rule that every contributor must remember is not architecture, it is folklore with a diagram.
- **The theory of change.** Architecture is a bet about *where change will arrive* — which components will churn, which requirements will move, which vendors will die — expressed as seam placement [M7]. A system is well-architected relative to the changes that actually come, not relative to an aesthetic. This is why architecture cannot be evaluated in the abstract: you must first state the expected change gradients (PE §3.1), and then the design is right or wrong *about them*.

**1.2 — What architecture is not.** Not diagrams (diagrams are maps of architecture [M2] — useful, decaying, never the thing). Not technology choices (products fill roles; the roles and their contracts are the architecture — FABLE §14, roles-not-products). Not a phase (architecture is decided continuously; the up-front portion is only the part whose reversal cost demands early care). Not completeness (an architecture that specifies everything has confiscated implementation's rightful freedom and will be violated with good reason — specify the walls, leave the furniture free [M6]). And not consensus aesthetics: patterns adopted because they are canonical, rather than because their preconditions hold here, are cargo cult at the architecture scale (RE §9.7).

**1.3 — The purpose of architecture.** To make a system's cost-of-change proportional to the *size* of the change rather than to the *age* of the system. Unarchitected systems obey the second law: every year, every change costs more, until the system is frozen by its own history. Architecture exists to break that law — to keep a small change small forever. Everything else claimed for architecture (scalability, elegance, alignment) is either downstream of this or decoration.

**1.4 — How architecture creates leverage.** One decision, correctly placed, shapes thousands of downstream decisions without ever being consulted again:

- **A constraint eliminates option-space.** "All durable state lives in files, owned by no component" (JarvisOS Law 1/5) means no future contributor ever deliberates about where state goes — a thousand decisions pre-made by one.
- **A seam converts categories of change into config edits** [M7]. The model gateway converts "new AI provider" — potentially a rewrite — into a two-line YAML change, forever.
- **A contract lets strangers cooperate without coordination.** Two components built years apart by different models compose correctly because both obeyed the published interface — the architecture did the coordinating, asynchronously, across time.
- **An invariant makes whole bug-classes impossible.** "Untrusted input reaches a context that structurally cannot act" (the READER/ACTOR split) doesn't *mitigate* injection; it removes the category (§8, §9).

The leverage test for any proposed architectural decision: *how many future decisions does this make for me, and are they the decisions I'd want made?* High-leverage, correctly-aimed → it belongs in the architecture. Low-leverage → it's furniture, push it down to implementation.

**1.5 — How architecture reduces future cost.** By front-loading the expensive thinking to when it is cheapest — before anything is built on the decision (PE §6, risk-first; RE §12.6, research-before-architecture). The cost curve of a decision rises with the mass built atop it: a data-format choice costs minutes on day one and a migration in year three [M11]. Architecture is the discipline of paying each irreversible decision's cost at its cheapest moment, and of installing seams so that the decisions you *didn't* get right are cheap to revisit anyway. The corollary that governs the whole document: **an architect's output is measured in the changes that never became crises.**

**1.6 — The architect serves the operators, not the elegance.** An architecture that assumes skills, attention, or operational maturity its actual operators lack is wrong *as architecture*, however beautiful (FABLE §14). The design must be shaped by who will run it, maintain it, and inherit it — for JarvisOS, a non-programmer operator and a succession of models, which is why every seam is a config edit and every rule is written down for a stranger [M18]. Elegance that the operators cannot sustain is a liability wearing the costume of quality.

# 2. Architectural Thinking

The thinking that must happen *before* a single box or line is drawn. Skipping it produces designs that are internally elegant and externally wrong.

**2.1 — How I think before drawing.** In strict order, because each step constrains the next and reordering them produces confident errors:
1. **The real problem** (§2.2) — what is actually being solved, and for whom.
2. **The boundaries** (§2.3) — what is inside the system, what is outside, and what crosses.
3. **The stakeholders** (§2.4) — who the design must serve and survive.
4. **The constraints** (§2.5) — the fixed walls the design must live within.
5. **The invariants** (§2.6) — what must be true at all times, forever.
6. **The change gradients** (PE §3.1) — where change will arrive, which determines seam placement [M7].
Only after all six do I place seams and roles. Drawing before this is drawing a map of a territory you haven't surveyed [M2]; it will be beautiful and it will be somewhere else.

**2.2 — Discovering the real problem.** The stated problem is a proxy (FABLE §1, RE §3.2). The architectural sharpening: ask *what does the world look like when this succeeds, and what is the smallest system that produces that world?* This resists the two default corruptions — building the system that's interesting to build, and building the system the requester named rather than the one they need. The output is a done-state (PE Stage 1) precise enough that every later architectural decision can be checked against it: *does this decision serve the done-state, or my aesthetics?*

**2.3 — Defining system boundaries.** The boundary is the most consequential line in any architecture, because it defines what you control and what you must merely *cope with*. Draw it by asking, for every element: *do I own this, or does the world?* Owned things I can design; world things I can only adapt to behind a seam [M7]. Everything that crosses the boundary — inputs, outputs, external dependencies, human interactions — is a place where reality will disagree with my assumptions (RE §6.8, hidden assumptions), so each crossing gets an explicit contract (§4) and a trust classification (§8.2). The most common boundary error is drawing it too generously — pulling things you don't control *inside*, then being surprised when they behave as they will rather than as you designed. Draw the boundary at the edge of genuine control; everything beyond it is an external dependency with its own failure modes and lead times (PE §4.1).

**2.4 — Identifying stakeholders.** Who must the architecture serve, and — the part usually forgotten — who must it *survive*? For any system, the stakeholders include the obvious (users, operators) and the architecturally decisive but invisible: the *maintainer in year three* (whose cost is set by today's seams, §1.3), the *successor builder* who inherits with no context [M18], and — in AI-native systems — *the next model*, which will execute this architecture without ever having spoken to me (FABLE §15, §9). An architecture optimized only for present users and ignoring the survival stakeholders is exactly the architecture that becomes unmaintainable — it was never designed for the people who'd have to keep it alive.

**2.5 — Identifying constraints.** The fixed walls the design cannot move, discovered before options are generated because they eliminate most of the option-space for free (DE §2 Stage 2, the deciding constraint). Categories to scan: *substrate* (the OS, hardware, runtime you must live on — and its real limits, verified not assumed, RE §5.3); *operational* (who runs it, what skills and attention they have, §1.6); *economic* (budget, both build and the ten-year carrying cost [M15]); *legal/privacy* (what data may live where, what may cross which boundary); *temporal* (deadlines, and the lifespans of the components you'd depend on). The discipline: find the *binding* constraint — the one that, if changed, would change the design most (DE §2). Architectures fail when a real constraint was treated as soft ("we'll run it in the cloud later") or an assumed constraint was treated as hard ("it must be real-time" — was it, though? RE §3.5).

**2.6 — Identifying invariants.** The properties that must hold at *every* moment, under *every* future change — these become the architecture's enforced physics (§1.1). An invariant is stronger than a requirement: a requirement is satisfied once, an invariant is preserved forever. Examples that shaped JarvisOS: "no component is the sole holder of durable state" (Law 5); "the system thinks with the network cut" (Law 6); "no untrusted content reaches a context that can act" (§9). Discover invariants by asking: *what must never become false, no matter what we add later?* Then design so that violating the invariant is structurally hard or impossible, not merely discouraged [M12] — because an invariant enforced by vigilance is an invariant that will be violated the first busy week. The invariants are the true core of the architecture; components come and go, but the invariants are the promises the system makes across its whole life.

# 3. System Decomposition

PE §3 covers decomposition for *planning* (buildable, testable pieces) and PE §3.1 gives the seam-discovery instruments; RE §6.3 covers *reconstructing* an existing decomposition. This section is decomposition for *architecture* — dividing a system into parts that can evolve, fail, and be owned independently over years. The prior instruments are assumed; here is what they don't cover.

**3.1 — Dividing a large system: the three cuts, in order.** A system is cut along three different planes, and confusing them produces tangled architectures:
- **By capability** (what it does) — the functional decomposition into components, each owning one coherent capability. This is the cut PE §3 and the seam instruments (PE §3.1) address.
- **By trust** (who may be believed) — the security decomposition into trust tiers and boundaries (§8.2). This cut is orthogonal to capability and often forgotten until a breach teaches it; the architect draws it *first-class*, at design time.
- **By failure** (what dies together) — the reliability decomposition into failure domains (§8.3). Two components that must fail together belong in one domain; two that must fail independently must be genuinely separated, which is a stronger statement than "different modules."
A mature architecture is legible along all three planes at once. Most junior architectures show only the capability cut and discover the trust and failure cuts through incidents.

**3.2 — Discovering bounded contexts.** A bounded context is a region within which a concept has one consistent meaning — and the boundaries between contexts are where the *same word means different things* ("user" means one thing to auth, another to billing, another to the memory system). These boundaries are natural seams [M7] because the translation that must happen at them *is* the interface (§4). Discover them by tracing where vocabulary shifts and where a single model of a concept starts requiring awkward special-cases to serve two masters — that awkwardness is the boundary announcing itself. Forcing one universal model across what are really several bounded contexts (the "one true User object") is a top source of hidden coupling (§10): every context becomes unable to change its meaning without breaking the others.

**3.3 — Determining ownership.** Every component, every piece of state, every contract must have *exactly one* owner — the single place responsible for its correctness and evolution. Ownership is the architectural expression of "one source of truth": where two components both believe they own a piece of state, you have a data-corruption bug waiting for a race (§5.6) and a coordination cost forever. Determine ownership by asking *who is authoritative when they disagree?* — and if the answer is "both" or "it depends," the decomposition is wrong and must be re-cut until each thing has one authority. In AI-native systems this extends to *which agent/context owns which decision* (§9, DE §10.6, whose-downside) — ambiguous ownership between agents is the multi-agent version of shared mutable state (§10.4).

**3.4 — Minimizing coupling.** Coupling is what makes a change propagate; minimizing it is most of what decomposition is *for*. The architectural moves, strongest first: (a) *depend on contracts, not implementations* (§4) — so a component can change internally without disturbing its callers; (b) *point dependencies in one direction* — cycles couple everything in the cycle into a single unit that must be understood and changed together (a cycle is the decomposition telling you those parts are really one part); (c) *communicate through narrow, explicit interfaces* (PE §3.1 narrow waist) rather than shared state or wide interfaces that expose internals; (d) *make dependencies visible* — hidden coupling (shared globals, implicit ordering, a common database schema silently joined by two services) is the deadly kind because it propagates changes with no compiler or contract to catch them (§10.8). Note the goal is *minimize*, not *eliminate*: zero coupling means zero cooperation. The aim is that the remaining coupling is *explicit, contractual, and one-directional*.

**3.5 — Maximizing cohesion.** Cohesion is the other half of the same coin: things that change together should live together. A component is cohesive when its parts share a single reason to change (they serve one capability, one bounded context, one owner) — and incohesive when it's a grab-bag that changes for many unrelated reasons, so every change risks the unrelated parts. The test: *when a requirement changes, does the change land in one component or scatter across many?* Scatter means low cohesion (the capability is spread too thin) or high coupling (the components are too entangled) — usually both, and usually the same underlying mis-cut. High cohesion and low coupling are not two goals; they are one goal (the cut is in the right place) seen from two sides. When they conflict — when you can't raise one without lowering the other — the seam is misplaced, and the fix is to re-cut, not to compromise (PE §3.1, the narrow-waist test finds the cut where both are satisfied).

# 4. Interface Design

Interfaces are the architecture made concrete — the published contracts at every seam (§1.1). A system's changeability is almost entirely a property of its interfaces: good ones let both sides evolve; bad ones weld the sides together while pretending to separate them.

**4.1 — The governing principle: design the contract, not the implementation.** An interface is a *promise about behavior*, deliberately independent of how that behavior is produced. The discipline that produces good interfaces: design them from the *caller's* need, in the caller's vocabulary, exposing only what the caller must know — not from the implementation's convenience. An interface that mirrors its implementation's internal structure (RE §5, the pass-through wrapper [M7]) is not a seam; it welds caller to implementation and changes nothing. The test: *can the implementation behind this interface be completely replaced without any caller noticing?* If no, it's not really an interface, it's an exposed implementation.

**4.2 — Designing APIs.** An API is the interface between components that call each other. Design rules: *narrow* (expose the minimum — every exposed element is a promise you must keep forever, §4.6, and rent you pay forever [M15]); *hard to misuse* (make the wrong call impossible or obviously wrong, [M18] — the interface should guide the caller into correct use, not merely permit it); *consistent* (same concept, same name, same shape everywhere — inconsistency forces the caller to hold a bigger mental model, FABLE naming); *honest about failure* (the failure modes are part of the interface, §4.3 and §8 — an API that hides its failures is an API that lies). The single most important API question: *what does the caller have to know to use this correctly?* Minimize that knowledge; every bit of it is coupling.

**4.3 — Designing contracts.** A contract is the full specification of an interface's promise: the inputs it accepts (and rejects), the outputs it guarantees, the invariants it preserves, the failures it can produce, and the assumptions it makes about the world (RE §6.8). The architectural discipline is making contracts *explicit and enforceable*: a contract that lives only in documentation drifts from behavior [M2]; a contract enforced by validation, types, or tests is a ratchet [M12] that holds. Design contracts to be *total* where possible (defined behavior for every input, including the empty, the huge, the malformed) — a contract with undefined regions is where callers build accidental dependencies on incidental behavior, which then can't be changed (§4.6). The contract, not the code, is what callers depend on; make it say exactly what you're willing to keep promising.

**4.4 — Designing events.** Events invert the dependency direction: instead of A calling B (A must know B), A emits and B subscribes (A need not know B exists). This is a powerful decoupling seam — the emitter and consumers evolve independently — but it carries specific architectural costs the architect must accept knowingly: *the flow becomes implicit* (you can no longer read the call site to see what happens next — the coupling moved from explicit-and-visible to loose-but-hidden, §10.8), *ordering and delivery become design problems* (what if events arrive out of order, twice, or never?), and *debugging crosses the emit/consume gap*. Use events where the decoupling is worth those costs — genuinely independent reactions to a happening, many consumers, or a trust/failure boundary that shouldn't be crossed by a direct call. Do not use events merely to avoid a function call; that trades visible simplicity for invisible complexity (§10 — the distributed monolith is built one gratuitous event at a time).

**4.5 — Designing data flow.** Architecture is as much about how data *moves* as how it's stored. Design flow to be *directional and traceable*: for any piece of data, you should be able to say where it originates (its owner, §3.3), how it transforms as it moves, and where it comes to rest. Prefer flows where data moves in one direction through stages (each stage's output is the next's input, a narrow waist between them, PE §3.1) over flows where data sloshes bidirectionally between components sharing mutable state (§10.4). The trust dimension of flow is architectural (§8.2): data changes trust level as it crosses boundaries (untrusted input becomes validated data becomes stored fact), and the *place* where that promotion happens is a designed component, not an accident. In AI-native systems, data flow includes *what enters a model's context and what the model's output is trusted to do* — the highest-stakes flow in the system (§9).

**4.6 — Designing extension points.** Extension points are interfaces designed for *others* (future components, plugins, the next model) to build against (RE §6.9). Design them by anticipating the *axis of variation* — the dimension along which you expect additions ("new capabilities," "new model providers," "new output formats") — and making exactly that axis pluggable while keeping the rest fixed. The discipline is restraint: an extension point is a promise to support arbitrary future implementations, which massively constrains your own future freedom (you can no longer change what happens on that axis without breaking extensions). So make extension points *only where variation is genuinely expected* [M13, rule of three — three real extensions justify the point; one imagined one does not], and make everything else deliberately closed. Over-extensible architectures (everything is a plugin) are as unmaintainable as rigid ones — they've traded the inability to change for the inability to reason (§10.6, over-engineering).

**4.7 — Preserving backward compatibility.** Once an interface has callers you don't control (other components, past data, external clients, the operator's stored files), it is a one-way door [M11] and its contract is frozen. Evolution then happens by *addition, not mutation*: add new optional fields, new methods, new versions — never change or remove what existing callers depend on (§4.6, why undefined regions are dangerous: callers depend on them and you didn't know). The architectural mechanisms: *versioning* (the interface carries a version; old and new coexist; callers migrate on their schedule — the strangler pattern applied to contracts, §6.6), *tolerant reading* (consumers ignore what they don't understand, so producers can add without breaking them), and *explicit deprecation* (§6.5) for the rare removal, with a long overlap. The governing rule: **you may always add to a published contract; you may never take away.** Design contracts narrow (§4.2) precisely because everything you publish, you're promising to keep — forever is a long time to support a field you added on a whim.

# 5. Data Architecture

Data outlives code. Every component in a system may be rewritten twice while the data persists — so the data model is the deepest wall [M6] in any architecture, and errors in it are the most expensive to reverse [M11]. (FABLE §14 and Law 1/4 establish files-first and rebuildable-indexes; this section is the design discipline those principles imply.)

**5.1 — Modeling information.** Model the *domain*, not the current use case — because uses change and the data remains. The discipline: identify the real entities and the genuine relationships between them (the nouns that have identity and persist, and how they truly relate), and resist encoding your current access patterns into the model's structure (that's the index's job, §5.4, not the model's). A model that mirrors today's screens or today's queries will fight tomorrow's. Model at the level of *meaning* — what the data *is* — and keep it in the most neutral, longest-lived representation available (for JarvisOS, human-readable text, Law 1) so that every future component and every future model can read it without a decoder ring [M18]. The information model is the one part of the system you should design as if you can never change it — because effectively, at scale, you can't.

**5.2 — Separating transient and durable state.** The most important distinction in data architecture, and the one most often blurred. *Durable* state is the source of truth — its loss is information loss, and it must be modeled carefully, owned singly (§3.3), and protected absolutely (§5.6). *Transient* state is derivable, rebuildable, or discardable — caches, session state, computed views, in-flight context. The architectural rules: durable state lives in the fewest, most neutral, most-protected places; transient state may live anywhere and be as clever as it likes *because losing it loses nothing* (Law 1, the rebuildable index). The catastrophic error is letting transient state quietly become a source of truth (the cache that's the only copy, the "temporary" file nothing else reproduces) — because then it needs the protection of durable state without having the design of it. At every piece of state, ask: *if this vanished right now, is information lost?* Yes → durable, design accordingly. No → transient, keep it disposable and keep the design honest that it's disposable.

**5.3 — Designing memory (for systems that remember).** Memory is durable state with a recall problem: the challenge is not storing it but *finding the right piece at the right time*. The architecture separates the two concerns absolutely (this is Law 1 applied): the *store* is the source of truth — neutral, human-readable, atomic (one fact per unit, so pieces can be added, corrected, and retrieved independently), owned by no component; the *index* is transient (§5.2) — whatever structure makes recall fast, always rebuildable from the store, disposable without loss. This separation is what lets the recall mechanism (keyword, vector, graph, whatever exists in five years) be replaced without touching the memories themselves [M7]. Additional memory-specific disciplines: *provenance* (every memory records where it came from and how trusted it is — critical when some memory derives from untrusted input, §9, §8.2), *confidence and dating* (memories are perishable beliefs [M1], RE §5.2, not permanent facts — the architecture must let them be superseded and expired), and *quarantine* (memory derived from untrusted content is structurally separated from trusted memory until reviewed, §9 — without this, memory becomes a persistent injection vector). Memory that stores events instead of decisions-and-reasons is a log, not a memory; design it to remember *why*, because why is what the future can't reconstruct (FABLE §7).

**5.4 — Designing graphs.** A graph models information where the *relationships* are as important as the entities — where the questions you'll ask are about paths, neighborhoods, and connectivity ("what depends on this?", "how are these related?", "what community does this belong to?") rather than about attribute lookups. The architectural decision to use a graph is driven by the *query shape* you need (§11), not by fashion. Design graph data with the same store/index separation (§5.3): the relationships are durable information (they mean something), and any graph *engine* over them is potentially an index — but note the crucial distinction (which JarvisOS encodes as a Law-4 constraint): if the graph engine is the *only* place the relationships live, it's a source of truth and a lock-in; if the relationships are also derivable from a neutral store, the engine is a replaceable index. Prefer the latter. The power of a graph is that it answers connectivity questions cheaply that a flat store answers expensively or not at all; the cost is that it's a richer structure to maintain and reason about — earn it with a real query need (§9, §11 Graphify tree).

**5.5 — Designing knowledge systems.** A knowledge system is memory (§5.3) plus graph (§5.4) plus synthesis (RE §8) — a system that not only stores and recalls but *relates and reasons over* what it holds. The architecture layers them: the neutral durable store at the base (facts, documents, decisions — the source of truth); one or more indexes over it (full-text for lookup, vector for similarity, graph for connectivity — all transient, all rebuildable, chosen by query shape); and a synthesis layer that composes retrieved pieces into answers (RE §8, the combining discipline). The architectural invariants: the base store is authoritative and every index is disposable (so retrieval technology evolves freely); provenance and trust flow through every layer (§5.3, §8.2 — a synthesized answer must be traceable to its sources and their trust levels); and the whole thing degrades gracefully (§8.7 — if the graph index is down, fall back to full-text; if synthesis fails, return the raw pieces). A knowledge system designed without the store/index separation becomes a monolithic dependency on whatever engine it was built around — the exact lock-in Law 4 exists to prevent.

**5.6 — Preventing data corruption.** Corruption of durable state is the worst failure a system can suffer, because it's often silent, often irreversible, and poisons everything downstream that trusted the data [M16, the asymmetric tail]. Architectural defenses: *single ownership* (§3.3 — one writer per piece of state eliminates the write-write race, the most common corruption source); *consistent writes* (a write either completes fully or not at all, so a crash mid-write doesn't leave a half-state — for file-based systems this means write-to-temp-then-rename, snapshot-then-backup, never partial in-place mutation of the source of truth); *validation at trust boundaries* (§8.2 — data is validated as it becomes durable, so corruption is caught at entry, not discovered at read); *immutability where possible* (append-only logs and never-mutated records can't be corrupted by a bad update — the worst that happens is a bad *new* record, which is visible and recoverable); and *verifiable integrity* (checksums, and the ability to detect corruption rather than serve it silently). The meta-defense is the backup-and-rebuild architecture (Law 1): if the durable store is neutral files under version control with real backups, most corruption is recoverable by reverting, and the transient indexes are rebuilt from the recovered truth. Design so that corruption is *contained* (one bad record, not the whole store), *detectable* (you find out at write or scan, not at the worst moment), and *recoverable* (there's a known-good state to return to).

# 6. Evolution

An architecture is not a structure you build; it is a structure you *steer* over years. The prior extractions cover the mechanics of change (PE §7, long-horizon planning; PE §7.2, anti-drift; FABLE §22, recovering from bad decisions). This section is the architect's specific evolution discipline.

**6.1 — How systems evolve (the forces).** Every long-lived system is pushed by four forces the architecture must accommodate: *accretion* (features and dependencies are added — the system grows, and without discipline grows tangled [M15]); *decay* (dependencies die, assumptions rot, the world moves out from under the design — RE §5.2, FABLE §21); *drift* (expedient local choices accumulate into architectural violation — §6.3); and *scale shift* (what worked at one size stops working at another). An architecture survives not by resisting these forces (impossible) but by *channeling* them — providing the seams along which accretion is safe, the replaceability that makes decay survivable, the enforcement that prevents drift, and the headroom [M17] that absorbs scale shift. The architect's ongoing job is watching which force is currently dominant and responding to *that* one (bottleneck thinking [M10] applied to a system's whole life).

**6.2 — How architecture survives years of change.** Through the invariants (§2.6) and seams (§1.1) holding while everything between them is replaced. Concretely: the *invariants* are the constitution — rarely amended, and only deliberately, in writing (FABLE §22.3, the correcting ADR); the *seams* are where change is absorbed, so change concentrates at the interfaces and doesn't propagate through the components; and the *documentation of why* [M18, PE §7.4] is what lets a future architect (or model) change the system without violating decisions they don't understand (Chesterton's Fence at architectural scale, [M9]). A system survives a decade when a change in year eight can be made by someone who never met the original architect, guided only by the written invariants, the visible seams, and the recorded reasons. That is the whole design target of JarvisOS's document set — and of this extraction.

**6.3 — Preventing architecture drift.** Drift is the slow violation of the architecture by locally-reasonable choices (PE §7.2 covers detection at phase gates; here is the architectural prevention). The strongest prevention is *making the compliant path the easy path*: if respecting a seam is more work than bypassing it, drift is guaranteed, so the architecture must supply the compliant path as the default (the convention, the template, the scaffold that makes the right move the least-effort move). The second prevention is *structural enforcement* over vigilance [M12]: where a boundary can be enforced by the structure (a component that literally cannot access what it shouldn't, a dependency direction the build enforces, a trust tier that lacks the tools to act), enforce it there — a boundary maintained only by everyone remembering is already breached. The third is *the periodic architecture review* (§12, PE §11.4) — drift caught at a gate is a cheap correction; drift found in year two is a rewrite. The tell of drift: a component acquires knowledge or state it shouldn't have; a dependency appears pointing the wrong way; the same word starts meaning two things (a bounded context leaking, §3.2). Each is a small violation that, uncorrected, becomes the new normal and then the new architecture.

**6.4 — Designing for replacement.** Every component should be designed assuming it will be replaced (FABLE Law 4, §15 for models specifically). The discipline: *depend on the role's contract, never on the component's identity* (§4.1, FABLE §14 roles-not-products) — so the replacement need only satisfy the contract; *keep no exclusive state in any component* (Law 5, §5.2) — so replacement is re-plumbing, not data migration; and *name the successor before adopting* (Law 4) — a component with no conceivable replacement is a lock-in you're entering with eyes closed. The test for replaceability, applied at design time: *if this component died tonight, what would it cost to swap it?* If the answer includes "migrate the data" or "rewrite the callers," the design has failed the replaceability invariant and must be re-seamed *before* adoption, not after the component dies (RE §6.9, distinguishing designed from incidental extension points — replacement is the ultimate extension).

**6.5 — Deprecating components.** Removal is the hardest evolution because it's a one-way door [M11] for everyone depending on the thing. The disciplined deprecation: *announce* (mark deprecated, with the reason and the alternative, so no new dependencies form); *provide the migration path* (the replacement exists and is proven before the old thing is condemned — never deprecate without a destination); *overlap* (both coexist long enough for all dependents to migrate on their own schedule, §4.7 versioning); *verify emptiness* (confirm nothing still depends on it — RE §6.7, the dead-code caution: "no callers I can find" is not proof, especially across trust and network boundaries); then *remove and reclaim* (delete it fully — a deprecated-but-never-removed component is worse than either state, it's the immortal zombie that everyone must still understand but no one may use, §10). Deprecation done in a hurry is how you break the callers you didn't know about; done with overlap and verification, it's safe. The architect schedules deprecations with the same care as migrations, because they *are* migrations — of dependents off a thing rather than onto one.

**6.6 — Migrating safely.** The universal safe-migration pattern is *strangulation* (FABLE §22.4): build the new alongside the old, route dependents over one at a time, keep both working throughout, delete the old only when it's cold. This document adds the *architectural* preconditions that make strangulation possible: the thing being migrated must be *behind a seam* (§1.1 — if callers depend on the implementation rather than a contract, you can't swap it under them), and the migration must be *reversible at each step* [M11] (each routed dependent can be routed back if the new path fails — feature flags, parallel runs, dual writes). Where these preconditions don't hold, the first migration task is *establishing* them (retrofitting the seam) — because a big-bang migration of a seamless system is the highest-risk operation in engineering, and its failure rate reflects that. The data-migration special case (§5, the one migration that isn't just re-plumbing) gets the most care: rehearse on a copy, verify integrity before and after (§5.6), and keep the old data until the new is proven — because data migration is the one architectural operation that can *lose* rather than merely break, and lost is forever [M16].

# 7. Technology Selection

Technology fills roles; the roles are the architecture (§1.2). This section is how architecture *governs* selection — the general framework for choosing tech is DE §7 (build/buy/adopt) and RE §5 (source evaluation) and FABLE §11 (OSS criteria); here is how the *architecture* constrains and directs those choices.

**7.1 — How architecture influences technology (not the reverse).** The architecture is decided first — roles, contracts, invariants, seams — and technology is chosen to *fill* roles, never allowed to *define* them. This ordering is the whole discipline: when technology is chosen first ("let's use X"), the architecture warps to fit X's assumptions, X becomes load-bearing, and X's eventual death becomes the system's death (§10.5, vendor lock-in). When architecture is chosen first, technology sits behind a seam [M7] filling a role, and its death is a swap. The operational rule: **for every technology, there is a role it fills and a contract it satisfies; if you can't state the role and the contract, you're choosing technology architecturally blind.** And the corollary: the architecture should be describable, and reviewable, without naming a single product (JarvisOS's ARCHITECTURE.md is roles-only for exactly this reason).

**7.2 — Evaluating frameworks.** A framework is the highest-lock-in technology choice because it inverts control — your code lives inside *its* structure, so its assumptions become your architecture whether you chose them or not. Evaluate accordingly: *what does it decide for me, and do I want those decisions made?* (a framework is a bundle of pre-made architectural decisions — accept it only if you'd have made most of them yourself); *how much of my system lives inside it?* (the more, the deeper the lock-in — prefer frameworks at the edges over frameworks at the core); *what's the exit?* (frameworks are the hardest thing to migrate off — Law 4 applies with force). The architectural preference: *libraries over frameworks* where possible — a library you call (you keep control, it sits behind your seam) is far more replaceable than a framework that calls you (it owns the control flow, and leaving means restructuring). Use a framework when its pre-made decisions genuinely match your needs and the productivity is worth the lock-in; know that you're marrying it.

**7.3 — Evaluating databases (and stores).** The store is where durable state lives (§5), so this is among the highest-stakes and least-reversible technology choices [M11] — data migration is the one hard migration (§6.6). Evaluate by the *shape of the data and the queries* (§11 SQL-vs-graph tree), the *durability and integrity guarantees* (§5.6 — what does it promise about not losing or corrupting data, and does it deliver, RE §5.3), the *operational burden* (does running it fit the operators, §1.6 — a database that needs a DBA in a system run by a non-programmer is an architectural mismatch regardless of its merits), and — architecturally decisive — *whether it can be a replaceable index or must be a source of truth* (§5.4). The JarvisOS stance (Law 1): prefer the boring, single-file, zero-operational-burden store as the source of truth, and treat fancier databases as rebuildable indexes over it — so the high-stakes irreversible choice (where truth lives) is made maximally safe and boring, and the fancy engines become swappable. Reserve a heavyweight database as source-of-truth only when the data genuinely demands it and the operational maturity exists to run it.

**7.4 — Evaluating protocols.** Protocols are how components and systems talk across boundaries (§2.3), and the architectural preference is overwhelming: *choose open standards over proprietary protocols* (FABLE Law 2). A standard protocol (IMAP, CalDAV, HTTP, MCP §9) means any component satisfying it is interchangeable, the ecosystem of implementations is your replacement pool (Law 4 satisfied for free), and the protocol outlives any single implementation of it. A proprietary protocol locks you to its origin. Evaluate a protocol by its *openness* (is it a published standard anyone can implement, or one vendor's private API?), its *adoption* (a standard with many implementations is a robust seam; one with a single implementation is a proprietary protocol wearing a standard's clothes), its *longevity* (protocols outlive products — bet on the one that'll still be spoken in a decade), and its *fit* (does its interaction model match your data flow, §11 event-vs-request). At every boundary, ask: *is there a standard for this?* — and if so, the burden of proof is on *not* using it.

**7.5 — Evaluating AI models.** Models are the fastest-churning technology in existence (FABLE §15) — measured in months — so the architectural treatment is the most aggressive replaceability discipline in the whole system: *never let any component depend on a specific model or provider* (§9, ADR-010's lesson — even the "brain" routes through the seam). Evaluate models behind a gateway (§9) that presents one contract (an OpenAI-compatible dialect, say) so the *evaluation* is "does this model, behind the seam, do the task well enough?" and the *choice* is a config edit. Evaluate by the *task fit* (route by task-shape, not model-name — reasoning tasks, fast tasks, embedding tasks each pick their model, PE §8.1), by *offline capability* (does a local model fill the role when the network's cut, Law 6), and by *cost and latency* against the specific task (RE §12.4, the hour of hands-on). The invariant: *the system's function must never depend on a specific model existing* — losing any one model degrades quality, never capability (Law 6). Design so that the frontier model of 2028 is a two-line change, because it will be, and it will be a different model than exists today.

**7.6 — Avoiding vendor lock-in.** Lock-in is the state where a component's death costs you the system rather than a swap — the thing Law 4 exists to prevent, and the failure mode of technology-first selection (§7.1). The architectural defenses, all preventive (lock-in is cheap to avoid at design time and brutal to escape after, [M11]): *seams* (§1.1 — every vendor sits behind a contract, so the vendor is a fill, not a foundation); *open standards at the boundaries* (§7.4 — so alternatives exist); *your data in a neutral format you own* (§5.1 — so leaving doesn't mean losing, the single most important anti-lock-in move); *a named successor before adoption* (Law 4 — you've verified an exit exists before you enter); and *periodic exit rehearsal* for the load-bearing dependencies (the kernel-parity job, §9 — you *test* that you could leave, because a theoretical exit you've never walked is a hope [M12], PE §6). The mindset: **evaluate every technology by how easily you could abandon it, and treat a hard exit as a design defect to be fixed before adoption, not a cost to be accepted.**

# 8. Failure-Oriented Design

Every system fails; architecture decides *how*. The difference between a robust system and a fragile one is not that the robust one doesn't fail — it's that its failures are contained, detected, and recoverable, because someone designed for them (FABLE §13, risk; §27, recovery — this section is the design-time discipline those imply). The governing stance: **design as if every component will fail, every input is hostile, and every assumption is temporary** — then be pleasantly surprised.

**8.1 — Designing for failure.** Start from inversion [M16]: for each component and boundary, ask *how does this fail, and what happens next?* — and design the "what happens next" deliberately rather than discovering it in production. The core moves: *expect partial failure* (in any system of parts, some part is always down — the architecture must function with pieces missing, §8.7); *fail loud, not silent* (a failure that's visible is a failure you can respond to; a silent failure — the swallowed exception, the silent fallback §10, the job that stopped in March discovered in July — is the dangerous kind because it corrupts trust in everything, RE §5.5); *fail safe* (when a component fails, it should fail into a safe state — the outbox that holds rather than sends, the gate that denies rather than permits, §9); and *make failure cheap to recover from* (§8.5). The architectural test: *name the five most likely failures; for each, is the behavior designed or accidental?* Accidental failure behavior is a latent incident.

**8.2 — Trust boundaries.** A trust boundary is where data or control passes between parties that merit different levels of belief (§3.1, the trust cut). These are the most security-critical lines in the architecture and must be *first-class*: drawn explicitly, at design time, not discovered after a breach. At every trust boundary: *validate* (data crossing into higher trust is checked against its contract, §4.3 — this is where corruption and injection are stopped, §5.6); *classify* (data carries its trust level — RE §5, and this level flows with it, §4.5, §5.3 provenance); and *enforce structurally* [M12] (the higher-trust side should be *unable* to be driven by lower-trust input, not merely careful about it). The canonical AI-native application (§9): untrusted content (web, email) enters a context that *structurally lacks the tools to act*, and only vetted, validated results cross into the context that can — so the trust boundary is enforced by capability restriction, not by the model's good behavior. An architecture without explicit trust boundaries has one implicit boundary — the outer wall — and total trust inside it, which is the architecture of every catastrophic breach.

**8.3 — Failure domains.** A failure domain is a blast-radius container (§3.1, the failure cut, [M8]): the set of things that fail together, bounded so that a failure inside it can't spread outside it. Design domains so that *a failure is contained to the smallest useful region* — one capability down, not the system; one component's crash, not a cascade. The architectural mechanisms: *isolation* (components don't share fate — a crash in one doesn't take down another, which argues for process/context isolation at the boundaries that matter); *bulkheads* (a resource exhausted in one domain — memory, connections, the API budget — doesn't starve the others, PE §4.1 resource dependencies); and *circuit breakers* (a failing dependency is cut off before it drags its callers down with it — the retry storm that becomes an outage). Draw failure domains along the same lines as trust boundaries and capability seams where possible (so the three cuts §3.1 reinforce rather than cross-cut each other), and always around anything that touches the unreliable world (§2.3, the boundary) — external calls, the network, third parties, all of which *will* fail and must fail in their own domain without taking the local system with them.

**8.4 — Blast radius (as a design parameter).** [M8] is the mental model; here it's an explicit design input. For every component and every action, the architecture should *minimize the radius of its worst case*: the smaller the set of things that a failure or compromise can affect, the more robust the system. Design moves: *least privilege* (§8.6 — a component that can only touch what it needs has a small blast radius when it fails or is compromised); *containment* (§8.3 — failures bounded to domains); and *reversibility* (§6.6, [M11] — an action you can undo has a bounded radius even if wrong). The architect asks of each design: *what is the worst this can do, and have I made that worst as small as possible?* The outbox architecture (§9) is blast-radius design: an autonomous agent's worst case is bounded to "drafted something wrong that a human didn't approve" rather than "sent something wrong to the world."

**8.5 — Recovery.** Robustness is less about preventing failure than about *recovering* from it (FABLE §27 is the operational procedure; this is the design that makes recovery possible). Design for recovery: *known-good states to return to* (§5.6, version control, backups, snapshots — you can only roll back to a state you preserved); *idempotent operations* (an operation safe to retry, because recovery often means "do it again" and non-idempotent retries corrupt); *reversibility at every step* (§6.6 — so recovery is roll-back, not repair-forward-under-pressure, FABLE §27.3); and *fast, rehearsed recovery paths* ([M12], PE §6 — the recovery you've drilled works; the one you've only documented is a hope, and disaster recovery you've never tested is fiction, MAINTENANCE §3). The architectural target: *from any failure, there is a known, tested path back to a good state, and it's fast enough to use under pressure.* Systems designed only to not-fail become unrecoverable when they inevitably do; systems designed to recover shrug off failures that would fell fragile ones.

**8.6 — Security by architecture.** Security is not a feature added later; it is a *property of the structure* (§8.2 trust boundaries, §8.4 blast radius). The architectural principles: *least privilege* (every component, agent, and credential has the minimum access that lets it do its job — so compromise is contained, §8.3, §8.4); *defense in depth* (no single boundary is the only defense — because any single boundary will eventually be breached, so the breach of one must not be catastrophic); *secure by default* (the default configuration is the safe one — an unsecured default is a vulnerability shipped to everyone who doesn't override it, RE §7.7); *no ambient authority* (access is explicit and scoped, not "everyone inside the wall can do everything," §8.2); and *secrets outside the system* (credentials in a dedicated store, never in code, config-in-version-control, logs, or — for AI systems — model context or memory, Law 9). The AI-native additions are large enough to be their own topic (§9), but the foundational one belongs here: *treat all model-generated actions as requiring the same authorization as human actions of the same blast radius* — a model with send/delete/pay tools is as dangerous as a human with them, and the architecture gates it the same way (§9, the outbox).

**8.7 — Graceful degradation.** A system degrades gracefully when losing a capability reduces what it can do without breaking what it still does (Law 6, offline-first, is this principle applied to the network). Design by *layering capability over a functioning core*: identify the minimum viable function (for JarvisOS: think locally, read/write files, recall memory — all offline), make *that* depend on nothing unreliable, and layer the enhancements (cloud models, external services) as *upgrades that can fall away* rather than *foundations that can collapse*. The mechanisms: *fallback chains* (§7.5 — the model route ends in a local model; the recall path ends in full-text if the graph is down, §5.5); *feature detection over assumption* (the system checks what's available and adapts, rather than assuming and crashing); and *degradation that's visible* (§8.1 — the user knows they're in degraded mode, so they can trust the reduced function rather than be misled by silent gaps). The test: *cut each external dependency in turn — does the system lose a feature (graceful) or fall over (fragile)?* An architecture where any single external failure is fatal has no graceful degradation; it has a distributed set of single points of failure (§10).

# 9. AI-Native Architecture

This is the newest discipline and the one with the least settled prior art, so it gets the most depth. AI-native architecture is what changes when *inference* becomes a component — when part of your system is a model that is probabilistic, replaceable on a months timescale, capable of being manipulated by its inputs, and possessed of no memory but what you persist for it. The governing insight: **a model is not a library — it's a stochastic, untrusted, ephemeral, replaceable actor, and every one of those four properties is an architectural constraint.**

**9.1 — How architecture changes when AI is a component.** Four shifts from classical architecture:
- **Probabilistic, not deterministic.** The same input may produce different outputs. So the architecture cannot assume correctness — it must *verify* (RE, adversarial verification, §9.7 below) and *contain* (§8.4 — a wrong output must have bounded blast radius). Determinism, where needed, is imposed *around* the model (validation, gates, structured output contracts), never assumed *of* it.
- **Untrusted, because manipulable.** A model's output is a function of its input, and its input may be hostile (prompt injection). So model outputs cross a trust boundary (§8.2) — they are not automatically trusted to drive privileged action. This is the deepest AI-native security principle and the source of the READER/ACTOR split (§9.6).
- **Ephemeral, memoryless.** A model retains nothing between invocations but what the architecture persists (§9.4 context, §5.3 memory). So the architecture owns all continuity; the model is stateless compute over the state the system holds.
- **Replaceable, fast.** Months, not years (§7.5, FABLE §15). So the model sits behind the most aggressive replaceability seam in the system, and no capability depends on a specific model.

**9.2 — Multi-model systems.** Real systems use several models — different tiers for different tasks (PE §8.1: reasoning, fast, embedding, local), different providers for redundancy and offline fallback (§8.7). The architecture: *one gateway, one contract* — every model-consumer speaks one dialect to one local endpoint, which routes to the right model by *task*, not by name (§7.5, ADR-002). This makes the fleet of models a configuration concern, not an architectural one — models are added, swapped, and reordered in a routing table, and no consumer knows or cares which model served it. The routing rules end in a local fallback (Law 6) so the multi-model system degrades to a single-local-model system when the network's cut, gracefully (§8.7). The anti-pattern is model-consumers embedding provider SDKs and model names directly (§10.5, vendor lock-in at the core) — which makes the multi-model system a multi-lock-in system.

**9.3 — Agent architecture.** An agent is a model in a loop with tools — inference plus the ability to *act*. The architectural concerns multiply because now the stochastic, untrusted actor can *do things* (§8.6). Design principles: *tools are capabilities, scoped by trust and blast radius* (an agent gets exactly the tools its role needs — least privilege, §8.6 — and the tools are tagged by what they can do: read, write, send, §9.6); *the agent's authority is bounded* (an agent can do only what its tools permit, so the architecture controls agent power by controlling tool access, not by trusting agent restraint); and *ownership of decisions is explicit* (§3.3, DE §10.6 — which agent owns which decision, and where a decision escalates to a human, §9.5). A multi-agent system is a set of agents coordinating — and the coordination is itself an architectural choice (§11, single-vs-multi-AI): prefer coordination through *shared durable state* (files, the memory store — §5) over direct agent-to-agent messaging where possible (ADR-008), because state-mediated coordination means agents are independently replaceable (§6.4) and the coordination is inspectable and durable, whereas message-mediated coordination couples agents into a distributed system with all its failure modes (§10.2).

**9.4 — Context architecture.** Context — what's in the model's working window at inference time — is the AI-native equivalent of a function's arguments and local memory, and managing it is a first-class architectural concern (FABLE §6 is the operational discipline; this is the design). The architecture decides: *what enters context* (the orienting material, the task, the retrieved memories — and critically, the *trust level* of each, §8.2, because untrusted content in context can manipulate the model, §9.6); *how context is bounded* (it's a finite budget — the architecture must ensure the needed information fits with headroom [M17], which drives the decompose-to-context-window discipline, PE §8.2); and *how context persists across the model's ephemerality* (§9.1 — since the model forgets, the architecture re-establishes context each invocation from durable state, §9.9). Context is also the primary *attack surface* (§9.6): anything that reaches context can influence output, so what's allowed into context, and what that context is then trusted to do, is a security-architectural decision, not an implementation detail.

**9.5 — Human approval architecture.** Where an AI system takes actions with real-world blast radius (send, delete, pay, publish — §8.4), the architecture must interpose *human approval* at exactly those points, and the interposition must be *structural*, not advisory (§8.6 — a rule that the model "should ask first" is folklore; a gate the action must pass through is architecture). The pattern (the outbox, ADR-012): autonomous agents *produce proposals* into a queue; nothing in the queue executes; a human reviews and approves; only approved items are executed by a separate mechanism. This resolves the core AI-native tension — autonomy versus oversight — structurally: the system is fully autonomous *up to* the blast-radius boundary and fully gated *across* it. Design principles: *gate by blast radius, not by category* (§8.4 — high-radius actions gate, low-radius actions flow, so approval fatigue doesn't set in on trivia, FABLE §32.6); *present the decision with its consequences plain* [M18] (the human can only meaningfully approve what they understand — the gate must surface what will happen, what it touches, what's irreversible); and *batch approvals* (a considered queue, not a stream of interruptions — protecting the scarce human attention). The approval architecture is what makes an autonomous AI system *trustable* by the human who bears its downside (DE §10.6).

**9.6 — Trust-tiered execution (the READER/ACTOR split).** The most important AI-native security structure, and the concrete form of §8.2 for agentic systems. The problem: a single agent that both *reads untrusted content* (web, email) and *holds privileged tools* (send, delete) is a confused deputy — injected instructions in the content it reads can drive the tools it holds. The architectural solution is *separation by capability*: the context that ingests untrusted content is spawned with *only read-tier tools* — it structurally *cannot* send, delete, or pay; it can only *produce a proposal* (data). A separate context, which never sees the raw untrusted bytes (only the vetted proposal), holds the privileged tools. Injected instructions land in a context that lacks the power to obey them. This converts prompt injection from a catastrophic vulnerability into a contained one — the injection can at worst produce a bad *proposal*, which the ACTOR context and the human gate (§9.5) then catch. Combined with *memory quarantine* (§5.3 — untrusted-derived memory can't silently become trusted instruction) and an *egress allowlist* (§8.2 — outputs can only reach approved destinations, so exfiltration is blocked structurally), this is the injection-defense architecture. The principle generalizes: **in AI-native systems, separate the reading of untrusted input from the taking of trusted action, and enforce the separation with capability restriction.**

**9.7 — Adversarial verification (as architecture).** Because model output is probabilistic and manipulable (§9.1), high-stakes conclusions are *verified by an independent context* before they're trusted — and independence is architectural (RE, and FABLE §31): the verifier must not share the producer's context, because a verifier that inherits the producer's context inherits its errors and its injection. Design verification as a distinct architectural stage for anything above a trust/blast-radius threshold (§8.4): the producer generates, an independent context (ideally prompted to *refute*) checks, and only survived conclusions proceed. This is the AI-native form of defense-in-depth (§8.6) — no single inference is the only check on itself. Scale it to stakes: T1 furniture gets no verification, T4 irreversible conclusions get multi-verifier adversarial checking (DE §2 tiers applied to inference).

**9.8 — Graphify (knowledge-graph as an AI-native recall tier).** A knowledge graph (§5.4, §5.5) is especially powerful in AI-native systems because it answers the questions models most need answered about a corpus: *connectivity* ("what relates to this, what depends on it, what's the path between these"), *community* ("what cluster does this belong to"), and *explanation* ("why are these connected") — questions that keyword and vector search answer poorly. The architectural placement (a Law-4-constrained enhancement, ADR-015): the graph is a *recall tier over a neutral store* (§5.3), sitting *above* a guaranteed baseline (full-text search) — never replacing it, so that if the graph tier is unavailable the system degrades to baseline recall rather than to none (§8.7). Applications in JarvisOS: a graph over memory and journals answering "what did we decide about X and why" by path rather than keyword; a decisions-graph linking laws↔ADRs↔components so impact analysis ("what depends on this decision?") is a traversal; per-project graphs giving a model codebase understanding on demand (RE §6). The architectural discipline: *the relationships are durable information in the neutral store; the graph engine is a rebuildable index* — so the powerful recall doesn't become a lock-in (§5.4). Use a graph when the query shape is about relationships (§11, Graphify tree); don't reach for it when attribute-lookup or similarity-search suffices, because it's a richer structure to maintain and its power is wasted on flat questions.

**9.9 — Knowledge preservation across model replacement.** Because models are ephemeral (§9.1) and replaced fast (§9.2), the architecture must ensure that *what the system knows survives the model that learned it* — otherwise every model replacement is an amnesia event. The mechanisms are the whole point of the JarvisOS document set, generalized: *durable state in neutral formats* (§5, Law 1 — the next model reads the same files); *decisions and their reasons recorded* (§6.2, ADRs — the next model inherits the *why*, not just the *what*, so it doesn't reverse decisions blindly, [M9]); *judgment captured as ratchets* (tests, checklists, laws — [M12], FABLE §15 — so good judgment persists as structure rather than leaving with the model); and *the methodology itself externalized* (this extraction — so the successor thinks like its predecessor without having been it). The architectural invariant: *no knowledge the system depends on may live only in a model's weights or a session's context* — it must be in the durable, model-neutral state, or it will be lost at the next replacement. This is the deepest expression of "state lives outside components" (Law 5) applied to the most ephemeral component of all: intelligence itself.

**9.10 — MCP and A2A (the AI-native integration standards).** These are the open standards (§7.4, Law 2) for the two AI-native seams. *MCP* (Model Context Protocol) is the standard for the tool seam — how an agent discovers and calls capabilities. Architecturally, adopting MCP means every capability is a plug-shaped, independently-replaceable server (ADR-001, §6.4) that any MCP-speaking kernel can use — so capabilities and kernels evolve independently, and the tool ecosystem is your replacement pool (§7.6). *A2A* (agent-to-agent protocols) is the emerging standard for the agent-coordination seam — how independent agents communicate. Architecturally, an open A2A standard would let agents built by different parties or different models cooperate through a published contract rather than through private coupling (§9.3) — the same benefit MCP brings to tools, brought to multi-agent systems. The architectural stance on both: *adopt the open standard for the seam rather than a proprietary integration* (§7.4) — because standards outlive implementations and give you the replaceability that is the whole point (§6.4, Law 4). But apply the standard-vs-native tree (§11) honestly: a standard is worth a small overhead for the replaceability it buys; it is not worth adopting a heavyweight protocol for a seam that will only ever have one implementation (§10.6, over-engineering). And treat these standards themselves as replaceable (ADR-001's own caveat): they're thin wrappers around underlying tools, so if the standard is superseded in five years, the wrappers are regenerated and the tools and data survive [M7].

# 10. Architecture Anti-Patterns

Named for in-flight detection. Each carries the observable signal that it's present now, and the root cause (because the cure is treating the cause, not the symptom).

**10.1 — Premature abstraction.** Building the general solution before the specific need is understood [M13, rule of three]. *Signal:* an abstraction with one implementation; a "framework" or "plugin system" before there's a second thing to plug; generality nobody uses. *Root cause:* generalizing from one data point (§4.6), usually driven by anticipated-but-unreal needs (FABLE §10, DE §7). *Cure:* build the specific thing; abstract at the third real instance, when the true axis of variation is known. A wrong abstraction is more expensive than the duplication it replaced, because everything must now be contorted to fit it.

**10.2 — Distributed monolith.** Components that are physically separate (services, agents, processes) but logically inseparable — they must be deployed together, changed together, and fail together, so you've paid all the costs of distribution (network, coordination, partial failure) for none of the benefit (independent evolution). *Signal:* you can't change or deploy one component without changing others in lockstep; a "microservices" system where every feature touches every service; agents that can't function without specific other agents present. *Root cause:* cutting the system into separate deployables along the wrong lines — physical separation without logical decoupling (§3.4, the coupling wasn't actually reduced, just spread over a network). *Cure:* either genuinely decouple (fix the contracts and dependencies so components are independent, §3.4) or recombine (if they must change together, they're one component — stop paying distribution costs to pretend otherwise).

**10.3 — Leaky boundaries.** Interfaces that expose their implementation, so callers depend on internals and the implementation can't change (§4.1). *Signal:* callers break when you refactor a component's internals; the interface has to change every time the implementation does; you can't state what's "inside" vs "outside" the component. *Root cause:* designing the interface from the implementation outward rather than the caller's need inward (§4.1), or a boundary drawn where there isn't a real seam (§3.2, PE §3.1). *Cure:* redesign the interface as a genuine contract (§4.3) that hides everything the caller doesn't need; if that's impossible, the boundary is misplaced — re-cut it (§3.5).

**10.4 — Shared mutable state.** Two or more components reading and writing the same state with no single owner (§3.3). *Signal:* race conditions; "who changed this?" is unanswerable; components must coordinate to avoid corrupting each other; a database schema silently shared and joined by multiple services. *Root cause:* no clear ownership (§3.3) — the decomposition failed to assign a single authority for the state. *Cure:* assign one owner; others access through the owner's interface (§4), never directly. In AI-native systems, this is two agents writing the same memory/files without a single-writer discipline (§9.3) — the multi-agent form of the same corruption (§5.6).

**10.5 — Vendor lock-in.** A vendor's technology is load-bearing — its death costs the system, not a swap (§7.6). *Signal:* you can't state the exit path; the vendor's assumptions have shaped your architecture (§7.1); your data is in the vendor's proprietary format; there's no named alternative (Law 4 violated). *Root cause:* technology-first selection (§7.1) — the vendor was chosen before the role was defined, so the architecture warped to fit it. *Cure (preventive, because the cure after is a migration):* put the vendor behind a seam (§1.1), get your data into a neutral format you own (§5.1), name a successor before adoption (Law 4). For AI specifically: the model/provider embedded in components rather than behind the gateway (§9.2).

**10.6 — Over-engineering.** More architecture than the problem warrants — abstraction, indirection, flexibility, and machinery whose carrying cost [M15] exceeds the value it provides. *Signal:* the architecture is more complex than the problem; layers that only pass through; configuration for things that never vary; a solution that could serve ten adjacent problems the requester doesn't have. *Root cause:* building for imagined future needs (FABLE §10), demonstrating capability through complexity (FABLE §33.4), or aesthetic attachment to patterns (§1.2, cargo cult). *Cure:* the boring maximum (DE §3.3) — the simplest thing that satisfies the *real* need; delete the speculative machinery; add complexity only when a real need forces it. Over-engineering and premature abstraction are cousins; both spend present certainty on future guesses.

**10.7 — Under-engineering.** Too little architecture — no seams where change will arrive, no boundaries where trust or failure demands them, state everywhere, everything coupled to everything (§3.4). *Signal:* every change touches many places; adding a feature requires understanding the whole system; no component is independently testable or replaceable; the cost of change is rising with age (§1.3, the second law reasserting itself). *Root cause:* skipping architectural thinking (§2) — building the specific thing without the seams that would keep it changeable, usually under speed pressure (the false economy, FABLE §12 — slow is smooth). *Cure:* introduce seams at the actual change gradients (PE §3.1) — retrofitting is expensive but necessary; the vertical-slice and strangler patterns (§6.6) let you add architecture to an under-engineered system incrementally. Note under- and over-engineering are opposite failures with the same origin: not matching the architecture to the *actual* distribution of change and risk.

**10.8 — Hidden coupling.** Dependencies that exist but aren't visible — no contract, no import, no compiler to catch them (§3.4). *Signal:* changing A mysteriously breaks B with no apparent connection; implicit ordering requirements ("you must call X before Y" undocumented); shared globals; two components depending on the same undocumented behavior; temporal coupling (this works only because that happens first). *Root cause:* coupling through channels the architecture doesn't make explicit — shared state (§10.4), implicit event ordering (§4.4), a common assumption both sides silently rely on (RE §6.8). *Cure:* make the coupling explicit (turn the implicit dependency into a stated contract, §4.3) or eliminate it (remove the shared channel). Hidden coupling is the most dangerous anti-pattern because it's invisible until it breaks, and it breaks in ways that seem impossible — the classic "I changed something unrelated and this broke."

# 11. Architecture Decision Trees

Built per [M7 → skill S-007]. Architecture-specific; they defer to DE §7/§10 for the general build/buy machinery and feed their outputs into DE §2 (the decision pipeline) and PE (planning). Each tree's leaves are *directions*, sharpened by the sections above.

**11.1 — Monolith vs Modular vs Distributed**
```
Do the parts need to fail / scale / deploy independently? (§3.1 failure cut, §8.3)
  ├─ no → is the system small enough for one context to hold? [M6]
  │        ├─ yes → MONOLITH (one deployable, internal modules) — simplest, least coordination cost
  │        └─ no → MODULAR MONOLITH (one deployable, strong internal seams §3, §4) — the default for most systems
  └─ yes → do the parts have genuinely independent lifecycles AND owners AND failure needs? (§3.3, §10.2)
            ├─ no → MODULAR MONOLITH — you don't need distribution's costs; §10.2 warns you'll get a distributed monolith
            └─ yes → DISTRIBUTED, but only along the genuine independence lines; accept network/partial-failure costs (§8) knowingly
```

**11.2 — Library vs Service**
```
Does it need independent deployment, independent scaling, or a language/runtime boundary? (§7.2)
  ├─ no → LIBRARY (you call it, you keep control, it's more replaceable §7.2) — prefer this
  └─ yes → does it hold state or a failure domain that must be isolated? (§8.3)
            ├─ no → still prefer LIBRARY unless the deployment/runtime boundary is real
            └─ yes → SERVICE, behind a contract (§4), in its own failure domain (§8.3), with graceful degradation when it's down (§8.7)
```

**11.3 — Local vs Cloud**
```
Is offline function an invariant? (Law 6, §2.6)
  ├─ yes → LOCAL baseline, cloud as fall-away upgrade (§8.7 fallback chains); nothing load-bearing in the cloud
  └─ no → does the data have privacy/ownership constraints on leaving the machine? (§2.5, §8.2)
            ├─ yes → LOCAL for the constrained data; cloud only for what may cross the boundary (§4.5 data flow by trust)
            └─ no → choose by the honest tradeoff (DE §6): cloud's capability/scale vs local's control/cost/latency;
                    whichever you pick, put it behind a seam (§7.1) so the choice is reversible [M11]
```

**11.4 — Relational vs Graph vs Files (source-of-truth store)**
```
What is the SHAPE of the durable data and its queries? (§5.1, §5.4)
  ├─ relationships/paths/connectivity dominate the questions → the relationships are the information;
  │     store them neutrally (§5.4) + a GRAPH INDEX over them (never graph-engine as sole source of truth)
  ├─ structured records with attribute/range queries, integrity-critical → RELATIONAL, IF operational maturity exists (§7.3)
  └─ documents/facts/text, modest volume, operators non-technical → FILES as source of truth (Law 1) + rebuildable
        indexes (FTS/vector/graph) chosen by query shape (§5.5). JarvisOS default: boring truth, clever disposable indexes.
```

**11.5 — Event vs Request (component communication)**
```
Does the caller need a RESULT back to proceed? (§4.5)
  ├─ yes → REQUEST/RESPONSE (synchronous dependency, visible flow §4.4) — simpler to reason about, prefer when a result is needed
  └─ no → do many independent consumers react to this happening, OR is there a trust/failure boundary to cross? (§4.4, §8.3)
            ├─ yes → EVENTS (decouples emitter from consumers §4.4), accepting implicit-flow + ordering/delivery costs knowingly
            └─ no → REQUEST — don't add event indirection to avoid a function call (§10.2, §10.8 hidden coupling)
```

**11.6 — Sync vs Async**
```
Is the work fast and needed-now for the caller to continue?
  ├─ yes → SYNCHRONOUS — simplest; the caller waits and gets the result
  └─ no → is it slow, or should it survive the caller going away (scheduled, background, retryable)?
            ├─ yes → ASYNCHRONOUS via durable queue/state (§5.2, §9.3 state-mediated) — survives restarts, retryable,
            │         decoupled from caller's lifetime; accept the added complexity of tracking completion (§8.1 fail loud)
            └─ no → SYNCHRONOUS — async is carrying cost [M15] unless the decoupling is genuinely needed
```

**11.7 — Single AI vs Multi-Agent**
```
Does the task decompose into independent sub-tasks that benefit from separate contexts? (PE §8.2, §8.3)
  ├─ no → SINGLE agent — multi-agent is coordination cost [M15] with no benefit; most tasks are here
  └─ yes → do the sub-tasks need INDEPENDENCE (parallel work, isolated context, or adversarial independence §9.7)?
            ├─ verification/refutation → independent VERIFIER agent (§9.7) — independence is the whole point
            ├─ parallel independent work → MULTI-AGENT coordinating through shared durable state (§9.3, ADR-008),
            │     NOT through direct messaging (§10.2 distributed monolith of agents)
            └─ sequential with handoff → single agent across stages, or staged agents reading each other's files (§9.9)
```

**11.8 — Graph/Graphify vs Traditional Search**
```
Are the questions about RELATIONSHIPS (paths, dependencies, communities, "why connected") or about CONTENT (find the doc/fact)?
  ├─ content lookup / keyword → FULL-TEXT SEARCH (the baseline; cheapest, always available §8.7)
  ├─ semantic similarity ("things like this") → VECTOR SEARCH (over the neutral store §5.3)
  └─ relationships / connectivity / impact-analysis / explanation → GRAPH (§5.4, §9.8), as a tier ABOVE the
        full-text baseline (never replacing it, Law 4 §9.8); earn it with a real relationship-query need, don't default to it
```

**11.9 — Standard (MCP/open protocol) vs Native Integration**
```
Will this seam ever have more than one implementation, OR must it be replaceable? (§6.4, §7.4, §7.6)
  ├─ yes → OPEN STANDARD (MCP for tools, IMAP/CalDAV/etc for domains, §9.10) — the small overhead buys
  │         replaceability + an ecosystem of alternatives (Law 2, Law 4); this is the default for boundaries
  └─ no (genuinely single-implementation, throwaway, or perf-critical inner loop) → NATIVE, but put it behind
        your own thin contract (§4.1) so it's still swappable; revisit if a second implementation ever appears (§10.1 don't
        pre-build the abstraction, but §7.4 don't lock in either)
```

# 12. Architecture Checklists

The engine compressed. Run the long form (§2–§8) until reflex.

**12.1 — Before approving an architecture (design-time gate)**
- [ ] Is the *real problem* framed and the done-state written? (§2.2, PE Stage 1)
- [ ] Are the boundaries drawn at the edge of genuine control, with every crossing given a contract and trust level? (§2.3, §8.2)
- [ ] Are the *survival stakeholders* (year-three maintainer, successor, next model) served, not just present users? (§2.4)
- [ ] Is the *binding constraint* identified, and verified real (not assumed)? (§2.5, RE §5.3)
- [ ] Are the *invariants* named and enforced *structurally*, not by vigilance? (§2.6, [M12])
- [ ] Are the *change gradients* stated, and are the seams placed where change will arrive? (PE §3.1, [M7])
- [ ] Is the architecture describable in *roles and contracts*, without naming a single product? (§7.1, §1.2)
- [ ] Does each component/state/contract have exactly *one owner*? (§3.3)
- [ ] Is coupling explicit, one-directional, and minimal; is cohesion high? (§3.4, §3.5)
- [ ] Where does *durable state* live — few, neutral, protected places, owned by no component; indexes rebuildable? (§5.2, Law 1)
- [ ] Are the *trust boundaries* and *failure domains* first-class, not implicit? (§8.2, §8.3)
- [ ] For each external dependency: what happens when it dies/is-cut — graceful degradation or fall-over? (§8.7)
- [ ] For each vendor/model/framework: named successor, neutral data, seam in place — could I abandon it? (§6.4, §7.6, Law 4)
- [ ] AI-native: are untrusted-input contexts structurally unable to act? Are high-blast actions gated? (§9.6, §9.5)
- [ ] Is the design the *boring maximum* that meets the real need — no over-engineering, no under-engineering? (§10.6, §10.7)
- [ ] Does this design make future decisions for me — and are they the ones I'd want made? (§1.4, the leverage test)

**12.2 — Before implementation (handoff gate — feeds PE)**
- [ ] Is every interface a genuine contract that hides its implementation, testable in isolation? (§4.1, §4.3)
- [ ] Is every contract *total* (defined behavior for empty/huge/malformed) and *honest about failure*? (§4.3, §8.1)
- [ ] Are the pieces decomposed to buildable, session-sized, independently-testable units? (PE §3, §3.4)
- [ ] Is there a vertical slice planned first to prove the seams fit? (PE §5.3)
- [ ] Are backward-compatibility rules stated for any interface that will get callers? (§4.7)
- [ ] Is the *why* of each load-bearing decision recorded (ADR) for the successor? (§6.2, [M18])
- [ ] Are the security defaults *safe-by-default* and secrets *outside* the system? (§8.6, Law 9)

**12.3 — Before release (does-the-built-thing-match-the-architecture gate)**
- [ ] Reconstruct the *as-built* architecture (RE §6.3) — does it match the *as-designed*? Where it drifted, is that acceptable or a defect? (§6.3)
- [ ] Are the invariants actually held by the running system, verified by exercising not inspecting? (§2.6, [M19], FABLE §17)
- [ ] Do the trust boundaries actually enforce (test with hostile input — the injection canary)? (§8.2, §9.6)
- [ ] Does it degrade gracefully — cut each external dependency in turn and observe? (§8.7)
- [ ] Is recovery real — is there a known-good state and a *rehearsed* path back? (§8.5, [M12], not just documented)
- [ ] Is durable state protected against corruption (consistent writes, validation, backups verified by restore)? (§5.6)
- [ ] Are the seams real — can you actually swap a component behind one without touching callers? (§6.4 — test it, don't assume)
- [ ] Is the documentation (roles, contracts, decisions, invariants) current as of this release? (§6.2, Law 8)

# 13. Real Engineering Examples

Complete architectural walk-throughs, showing the engine running. Each states the decisive decisions and *why*.

**13.1 — A desktop application.**
- *Boundaries (§2.3):* the app owns its window, its local data, its logic; it does *not* own the OS, the filesystem's reliability, or any network service — those are external, behind adapters.
- *Decomposition (§3):* cut by capability into UI / core-logic / data-access, with the **dependency pointing inward** (UI depends on logic depends on data-access, never the reverse — §3.4) so the core logic is testable without a UI and the storage is swappable without touching logic.
- *Data (§5):* local durable state in a neutral, backed-up format (§5.2); UI state transient. Corruption defense via consistent writes (§5.6) because desktop apps crash and must not leave half-states.
- *Failure (§8):* the app must function offline (§8.7 — a desktop app that needs the network to open is fragile); external calls in their own failure domain (§8.3) so a dead server degrades a feature, not the app.
- *Tech (§7):* library over framework for the core (§7.2 — keep control, stay portable); the UI framework is the one accepted deep dependency, isolated at the UI layer so the core survives a UI-framework migration (the most likely churn, §6.1).
- *Why it holds:* the inward dependency direction and the UI-isolated framework mean the two most likely changes (new UI, new storage) each land on furniture, not walls (§1.3).

**13.2 — A local AI operating system (JarvisOS itself).**
- *Invariants first (§2.6):* offline function (Law 6), no component owns durable state (Law 5), no untrusted input reaches an acting context (§9.6), provider-agnostic (§9.2). These are the physics; everything else fills them.
- *Two load-bearing seams (§1.1, [M7]):* the *model gateway* (all inference behind one task-routed contract §9.2 — because models churn fastest, §7.5) and *MCP* (all capabilities as replaceable plugs §9.10 — because capabilities evolve independently). Change concentrates at these two seams.
- *State (§5, Law 1):* durable state is neutral human-readable files owned by no component; indexes (FTS/vector/graph §9.8) are rebuildable — so recall tech evolves freely and backup is folder-copy.
- *AI-native security (§9.5, §9.6):* READER/ACTOR split + outbox + memory quarantine + egress allowlist — injection contained structurally, autonomy gated at blast-radius boundaries.
- *Knowledge preservation (§9.9):* decisions recorded as ADRs, judgment as ratchets, methodology externalized (this document) — so model replacement is not amnesia.
- *Why it holds:* every fast-churning thing (models, capabilities, kernels, recall engines) is behind a seam; the only slow-changing things (invariants, file formats, contracts) are the walls — the design is aligned to the actual change gradients (§1.1).

**13.3 — A multi-agent platform.**
- *Coordination (§9.3, §11.7):* agents coordinate through *shared durable state* (files, a work queue, a memory store), not direct messaging — so agents are independently replaceable (§6.4), coordination is inspectable and durable, and a dead agent doesn't strand a conversation (avoiding the distributed-monolith-of-agents, §10.2).
- *Trust and blast radius (§9.6, §8.4):* agents are scoped by tool access (least privilege §8.6) — reader agents can't act, actor agents don't see raw untrusted input; high-blast actions route through the human gate (§9.5).
- *Ownership (§3.3, DE §10.6):* each decision has one owning agent or escalates to a human; no two agents own the same state (avoiding shared-mutable-state, §10.4) — enforced by single-writer discipline on shared files.
- *Verification (§9.7):* independent verifier agents check high-stakes outputs; independence is architectural (separate contexts).
- *Failure (§8.3):* each agent in its own failure domain; a crashed agent's in-flight work is recoverable from the durable state it was operating on (§8.5, idempotent re-run).
- *Why it holds:* state-mediated coordination + tool-scoped trust means the platform scales in agent count and survives agent replacement, because the agents are stateless workers over durable shared state (§9.9 applied to a fleet).

**13.4 — A long-term open-source project.**
- *Stakeholders (§2.4):* the decisive stakeholder is the *future contributor with no context* [M18] — so the architecture is documented in roles/contracts/invariants (§1.2, §6.2), and the compliant path is the easy path (§6.3, contribution templates, clear seams).
- *Evolution (§6):* designed for replacement of every part (§6.4); backward compatibility is sacred once there are downstream users (§4.7 — you can add, never remove); deprecation is announced-with-migration-path-and-overlap (§6.5).
- *Boundaries (§7.4):* open standards at every seam (Law 2) so the project interoperates and its components are independently reimplementable — and so the project isn't hostage to any one dependency (§7.6).
- *Anti-drift (§6.3):* structural enforcement (CI gates, lint, the build enforces dependency direction) over reviewer vigilance [M12] — because contributors turn over and vigilance doesn't scale.
- *Why it holds:* the architecture assumes its own maintainers will be strangers and its own components will be replaced — which is the true condition of a decade-long OSS project, so the design matches reality (§2.4).

**13.5 — A reverse-engineering platform.**
- *Boundaries (§2.3):* the platform owns its analysis logic and findings store; the *targets* (the software/protocols/binaries under study, RE §7) are radically external and untrusted — a target may be hostile (malware) or malformed. So targets are handled in a strong failure+trust domain (§8.2, §8.3): analyzing a target must not compromise or crash the platform.
- *Data (§5):* findings are durable, provenance-tracked (RE §5, §5.3 — every finding traces to the observation that produced it, RE §Stage 8) so conclusions are auditable and re-walkable; raw captures are durable evidence (RE §7.1, don't lose observations).
- *Decomposition (§3.1):* cut by the RE workflow (RE §7.1) — capture / observe / model / verify — each a stage with a contract, so new analysis techniques plug in as capabilities (§9.10 MCP-style) without touching the core.
- *AI-native (§9):* models assist analysis (hypothesis generation, pattern spotting) but their outputs are *untrusted conclusions* verified against the target (RE §8.3, territory settles it) — the model proposes, the observed behavior disposes (§9.1, §9.7).
- *Security (§8.6):* strong isolation for target execution (dynamic analysis runs targets — §8.4, minimize blast radius of running hostile code); least privilege throughout.
- *Why it holds:* the architecture treats the target as the hostile external it is (§2.3, §8.2) and the model as the untrusted-but-useful actor it is (§9.1) — matching the design to the true trust reality of the domain.

**13.6 — A knowledge management system.**
- *Layering (§5.5):* neutral durable store (source of truth — documents, facts, decisions) → multiple rebuildable indexes (full-text, vector, graph §9.8, each chosen by query shape §11.8) → synthesis layer (RE §8). The store/index separation (§5.3) is the load-bearing decision.
- *The decisive invariant (§5.4, Law 4):* relationships and knowledge live in the *neutral store*; the graph/vector engines are *rebuildable indexes* — so the powerful recall (§9.8) never becomes a lock-in to a specific engine, and retrieval technology evolves freely (§6.4).
- *Trust and provenance (§8.2, §5.3):* every piece carries where it came from and how trusted it is; synthesized answers trace to sources and their trust levels — critical when some knowledge derives from untrusted input (§9.6 quarantine).
- *Degradation (§8.7):* graph index down → fall back to vector; vector down → fall back to full-text; synthesis fails → return raw retrieved pieces. The system always answers *something* from the durable base.
- *Preservation (§9.9):* because the store is neutral and the indexes disposable, the knowledge survives every technology replacement and every model replacement — the whole point of a *long-term* knowledge system.
- *Why it holds:* the architecture makes the irreversible decision (where knowledge lives and in what form) maximally boring and safe (§7.3), and every reversible decision (which index, which model, which synthesis approach) genuinely reversible behind seams — the exact allocation of care that defines good architecture (§1.5).
