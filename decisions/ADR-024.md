# ADR-024 — Availability-failover routing: every task route carries a three-tier fallback chain
**2026-07-10 · Accepted** — from the [2026-07-10 architecture review](../reviews/2026-07-10-architecture-review.md) §5.5–5.6 / §6; makes explicit what ADR-002 and ADR-005 implied.

**Context.** ADR-002 required fallback chains "ending in a local Ollama model" and ADR-005 made offline the baseline — but neither required an *alternate cloud provider* in the chain, so the written design degrades straight from frontier to local. June 2026 demonstrated the missing middle: Claude Fable 5 was suspended shortly after release under a government export directive and redeployed weeks later — a frontier model vanishing overnight for entirely non-technical reasons, while every *other* frontier provider kept serving. Meanwhile frontier convergence (the smallest top-tier quality gap on record) makes alternate-provider fallback nearly free in quality terms, and current open-weight models on this hardware (Qwen3.5-class on a 12 GB RTX 4070 Ti) make the local floor genuinely useful rather than dignity-preserving.

**Decision.**

1. **Every task route in `config\models.yaml` carries a three-tier chain:** primary frontier → **alternate-provider frontier** → local model. No route may have fewer than three tiers; the final tier is always local (ADR-005 stands).
2. Tiers are **provider-diverse by rule**: the alternate must be a different provider, not a different model from the same one — the failure mode being defended is provider-level (outage, policy, export control, account loss), not model-level.
3. Failover is **automatic at the gateway** (this is a routing-config obligation, not new software) and **logged**: the watchdog surfaces any day the system ran on a non-primary tier, so silent degradation is impossible and a dead primary is noticed the day it dies, not at the quarterly checkup.
4. **Model names live only in the living registry and `models.yaml`** — never in ADRs, skills, jobs, or kernel instructions (the engine corpus's own discipline, now binding on configuration). Routes are named by task (`reasoning`, `fast`, `embedding`, plus a property flag for offline-capable — resolving red-team M2's taxonomy mix), and the quarterly model review (MAINTENANCE.md §1.4) re-verifies all three tiers of every chain.

**Consequences.** The founding requirement — "everything continues working if one model disappears" — becomes a tested property of every route rather than a property of the architecture diagram. Cost: the operator holds API keys for at least two cloud providers (recorded in ACCESS-PREREQS with costs), and quarterly review covers three tiers per route instead of one.

**Alternatives considered.** (1) *Two tiers (frontier → local), as originally written* — rejected: June 2026 showed the common failure is one provider, and dropping from frontier to 9B-class for weeks is a quality cliff the middle tier removes for cents. (2) *Multi-provider load balancing (active-active)* — rejected: doubles spend and surface for a resilience the fallback chain already provides; this is a single-operator system, not a service with an SLA. (3) *A standing subscription to every provider* — rejected: COSTS.md discipline; keys can exist with near-zero standing spend, which is all the chain requires.

**Future review criteria.** Reopen if: (a) the watchdog shows a route running on its alternate or local tier for more than a week — the primary is de facto dead; promote deliberately and re-fill the chain; (b) frontier divergence returns (one provider pulls decisively ahead) — the quality cost of tier 2 rises and the chain composition warrants rethink, not removal; (c) local models reach frontier-adjacent quality on this hardware — consider inverting chains for private/offline-preferred tasks (local primary, cloud escalation), by new ADR.
